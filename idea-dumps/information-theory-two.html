Information Theory 2: What If Memory Could Travel as a Ripple?

A speculative journey from two vibrating electrons to a computer whose memory and processor exchange information through free-space electromagnetic fields.

Saumya Bhandari · 17 October 2026 · Thought experiment, not a demonstrated design

I was reading about the von Neumann bottleneck—how data travels between memory and a processor—and a question arrived that I couldn't quite leave alone: what if the memory did not have to send its bits along a conventional bus? What if it could ripple through physical space, and the CPU could listen?

To get there, I want to begin with something almost absurdly small: one electron, another electron, and the space between them. I will make the universe ideal first. Then I will add direction, information, distance, and finally the rough sketch of a computer.

Part I — The perfect system

One electron disturbs the quiet

Imagine a perfectly isolated enclosure with two charged particles, A and B, separated by empty space. I give A a small oscillatory push. Its acceleration changes the electromagnetic field; an outward disturbance propagates at the speed of light. After the travel time, B feels that change and begins to respond. Accelerating B then produces its own fields, some of which propagate back toward A.

For the first thought experiment, I will pretend the entire arrangement is lossless: no absorption, no thermal noise, no radiation escaping the enclosure, no friction, and no energy consumed by detection. I want to see what a perpetual exchange of motion would look like under these deliberately impossible simplifications.

This image already teaches me something. The field is not a messenger that must physically pick up an electron and carry it across the room. A charge at one position affects the field around it, and the disturbance changes the forces on another charge later. It is the change that travels.

But I must not smuggle in free energy. A charge that accelerates radiates, and accelerating a second charge requires energy. In real systems the back-reaction, boundary conditions and energy flow matter. A perfect lossless cavity can support idealized undamped electromagnetic modes, but two unsupported free charges do not automatically become a perpetual oscillator merely by exchanging radiation. In this section, the drive and the returning response are part of an imagined model.

Part II — The one-way layer

What if space had a turnstile?

Now I place an artificial layer between A and B. Let us imagine it transmits an electromagnetic wave traveling from A to B but rejects an otherwise matching wave traveling from B back to A. A moves; B feels its disturbance; B moves in response; B's backward radiation hits the layer and is turned away or absorbed.

There is an important distinction here: an ordinary polarizer selects a field orientation, not a travel direction. A one-way device would have to be a nonreciprocal isolator, potentially using a magnetic bias, time modulation, or nonlinear mechanisms under suitable conditions. It also cannot dispose of the rejected wave without an energy account: it must reflect, redirect, or absorb it, and some practical schemes require external power.

My instinct is to ask whether directional control would make electromagnetic signaling more useful for a computer. The answer might be yes—but it would not, by itself, provide isolation from crosstalk, precise addressing, or the ability to radiate millions of independent bits in the same space.

Part III — Encoding a message

Could a vibration say “I love you Trishna”?

Suppose we do not encode a bit as an electron permanently staying up or down. Instead we specify a reference clock and drive an antenna or charged structure with controlled oscillations. In a basic binary phase-shift keying model, one bit is a carrier with phase 0; the other is the same carrier shifted by π. At the receiver, coherent detection compares the incoming field with a synchronized reference and decides which bit was sent.

A crest alone is not universally “1” and a trough “0”: those alternate even in an unmodulated sine wave. The bit only exists relative to a sampling interval and a phase reference. This is how I can make the simple intuition mathematically meaningful.

sb(t) = A cos(2πfct + πb),   b ∈ {0, 1}

Let me send the phrase I love you Trishna. It is 18 ASCII characters here, encoded as 8-bit bytes—144 bits in total. The interactive field below animates the beginning of that very bitstream; the entire binary message remains visible beneath it.

That gives us a communication system, not yet a memory system. We still require an encoder, a stable oscillator, synchronization, receivers, detection thresholds, and error correction. The energy needed for a low-error bit is not magically zero merely because a field carries it.

Part IV — Speed-of-light transfer

Distance becomes delay

If the signal leaves A and travels along a direct path in free space, the ideal propagation delay is distance divided by the speed of light. A centimeter takes about 33 picoseconds; ten centimeters about 0.33 nanoseconds; a meter about 3.34 nanoseconds. These are propagation times only, not RAM access times or end-to-end transfer latencies.

I was initially tempted to call this superior to optical fiber because the wave goes in a straight line. But a fiber can also run along a nearly straight path, and electromagnetic disturbances on copper traces already propagate very quickly—commonly at a sizeable fraction of c. A free-space link might reduce some routing constraints, yet its antennas, modulation, path losses, interference, and converters could easily cost more time and energy than a wire.

And there is a deeper irony: memory performance is not mainly held back because electricity crawls along a wire. DRAM cell activation, queues, scheduling, serialization, processor caches, and the amount of data that can move at once all matter. Speed of propagation and speed of useful information delivery are not the same thing.

Part V — A computer that listens

RAM to CPU, without the usual conversation

Imagine RAM cells feeding an addressable RF or photonic modulator array. The processor requests a specific address; a controller selects the appropriate data; a transmitter assigns the data bits to electromagnetic symbols and launches them into a carefully controlled physical enclosure. On the other end, a receiver measures the field, synchronizes, detects the symbols, checks for errors, and reconstructs the original bytes for the CPU.

I could begin with a toy rule—upward electron motion means 1; downward means 0—but in a real alternating carrier, each electron reverses direction every cycle. So the receiver would need a specified instant, reference orientation, and symbol period. Phase modulation gives a cleaner first prototype. The moving charge is an implementation of a field transmitter, not a tiny courier carrying the bit to another electron.

The harder question is whether we can do more than exchange memory bytes. Can a carefully engineered electromagnetic mode store a state, and can a query excite a response that performs some computation as the wave propagates? Resonators can retain fields for finite times, and wave interference can implement useful analog operations. Persistent, dense, randomly addressable memory with reliable reads and writes is a very different challenge. Nothing in this essay demonstrates that conventional RAM can be replaced.

The reality check

What survives the thought experiment?

Much of this already has relatives in engineering: wireless links, integrated photonics, optical interconnects, coherent modulation, nonreciprocal components, resonators, and wave-based analog computing. It would be misleading to claim that transmitting information as electromagnetic waves is new. The more precise unanswered research question is whether an addressable, spatially compact field-based memory link can beat an electrical memory bus on latency, throughput, energy per bit, and reliability for some workload.

To test it, I would first build a single-channel tabletop demonstrator using a known bitstream, synchronized source and receiver, and a shielded or otherwise well-controlled channel. Next I would add a directional component and measure what happens to the return signal. Then I would compare against a wired reference using bit-error rate, bandwidth, end-to-end delay, and energy per delivered bit. Only then would multi-channel addressing, parallel reads, and memory semantics enter the discussion.

Possible failure modes: energy lost to radiation or absorption; interference between simultaneous channels; RF spectrum limits; poor antenna efficiency at chip-scale distances; synchronization overhead; security leakage; and the fact that a free-space radio path does not eliminate the RAM array's internal access delay.

So I keep the name Information Theory 2 as the title of a notebook entry, not as the claim of a replacement for Shannon. Classical information theory already describes electromagnetic communication very well. If there is a new idea here, it may belong to architecture, encoding, or wave-based computation. The mathematical theory will have to be earned.

Maybe the next computer will not move information between separate neighborhoods. Maybe it will learn to make the space between them part of the calculation.
