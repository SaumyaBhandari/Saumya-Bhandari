When Light Takes Time: From Einstein to the Double Slit

A clock tower, a train, a pair of tiny openings, and a question about whether matter could be guided by the world around it.

Saumya Bhandari · October 2026 · Illustrated essay

Picture yourself standing just two metres from a clock tower. The hands click to twelve. At that precise moment, the light carrying the image of those hands begins travelling towards you. When you see the hands at twelve, you are looking at an event that already happened.

For an ordinary clock tower the delay is tiny: about 6.7 billionths of a second across two metres. So, to make this intuition easy to see, let's imagine a universe where light travels much more slowly.

Our imaginary slow-light city. Set the speed of light to two metres per second. You stand two metres from the tower: the light arrives one second later. At the moment you see 12:00:00, the tower's clock reads 12:00:01. A friend standing one hundred metres from the tower sees the noon image fifty seconds after noon. The numbers are imaginary; the delay is real physics.

1. Looking is always looking backwards

The Sun we see is roughly eight minutes in the past. A distant star can appear as it was years ago. Every sight is a message from an earlier event. Light carries information, and information needs time to travel. That sets a limit on causality: something that happens here cannot affect a distant place sooner than a light signal could arrive.

Now imagine two flashes on opposite ends of a fast-moving train. Someone standing exactly midway between the flashes on the platform can receive both flashes together. A passenger travelling towards one flash and away from the other must deal with light reaching them differently. Correcting for light-travel delay does not make both observers agree on which distant events happened at the same time. Simultaneity itself depends on the observer's motion.

2. Why a moving ruler looks shorter

Einstein began with two demands: the laws of physics work the same in every uniformly moving laboratory, and every such observer measures the same speed of light in vacuum. Imagine a clock made from a beam of light bouncing between two mirrors. Viewed from the side of a moving train, the light travels a diagonal route between ticks. That route is longer, but its speed remains the same. The moving clock therefore ticks more slowly according to a platform observer.

The story also works in reverse: each inertial observer finds the other observer's moving clocks slow. To keep the speed of light and the measurements of space and time consistent, distances measured along the direction of relative motion must change as well. A ruler resting inside the train has its normal length to passengers; observers on the platform measure a shorter distance between its ends when they record both endpoints at the same platform time.

\[L=\frac{L_0}{\gamma},\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}\]

If the train moves at 80% of light speed, its longitudinal length in the platform frame is 60% of its rest length. Nobody on board feels squashed. The measured distance changes because observers divide spacetime into “now” and “elsewhere” differently.

3. What happens if that moving object is an electron?

Electrons are tiny charged particles. At ordinary speeds we can model their motion approximately with classical trajectories. At relativistic speeds we must account for special relativity and, for precise predictions, quantum mechanics. The metal around a slit contains atoms and electrons; it can exert real electromagnetic forces on a passing charge. Viewed from the electron's rest frame, the plate is contracted along the direction of motion. That is a real geometric ingredient worth studying.

But contraction does not magically turn the plate into a perfect, noiseless sheet. Its electric and magnetic fields transform too, and a rapidly traversed material may still scatter or exchange energy with an electron.

4. Two openings, one electron at a time

Imagine throwing tiny grains of sand towards a wall pierced with two narrow openings. Every grain passes through one hole or the other. Behind the wall you expect two blurred piles. Now replace the grains with electrons, and make the experimental conditions coherent. A sensitive detector registers each electron as one dot, yet after many electrons the dots form alternating bright and dark stripes.

In the standard calculation, there are complex probability amplitudes for alternatives through the apparatus. The amplitudes combine, and their squared magnitude predicts where a detector is likely to click. At a dark stripe, contributions cancel. This does not require two electrons to collide with one another; the pattern survives when electrons arrive individually.

5. Could the metal around the openings shape the trajectories?

Here is the question behind my relativistic plasma-lensing idea. A slit plate is made of matter. It contains charges and fields. Perhaps, at sufficiently high electron energies and carefully chosen materials, its electromagnetic structure can deflect electron trajectories and produce focusing patterns on a screen. In optics, many rays can crowd into bright curves called caustics. Think of the bright lines on the bottom of a swimming pool.

I want to test whether a relativistically moving electron encountering a metal slit could experience a structured electromagnetic lens. It would involve the atomic potential, electronic response, the flight time through the material, and how a bundle of possible impact points bends towards a screen. A useful theory would have to calculate the positions of the bands, their spacing, and their strengths.

6. The beautiful part of the mathematics—and its limit

Classical mechanics has a quantity called action, roughly a measure accumulated along a route. Quantum mechanics associates a phase with that action. Different routes can carry different phases. When the phases are added as complex amplitudes, dark and bright fringes follow naturally. Catastrophe optics connects the geometry of focused classical paths with Airy and Pearcey wave patterns.

That connection is established and useful. But adding complex phases and squaring their total to get probabilities is a quantum rule; it has not been derived from classical particles alone by the plasma-lensing proposal. And an electron can interfere in setups that do not use a relativistic metal slit. Any broader explanation must confront those experiments too.

7. What would convince me?

A strong test would compare two independently calculated screen patterns: one from the proposed classical plasma-lensing forces and an initial distribution of point-particle positions, and the other from quantum mechanics. Then alter the plate material, temperature, electron energy, and slit geometry. If the classical model predicts quantitative fringes, including nearly empty dark bands, without inserting quantum amplitude superposition by hand, it would be a substantial result. If it cannot, the classical lens may still account for useful corrections or focusing effects.

Every image we see is a message from the past. Every electron detection is a tiny event in the present. What interests me is how much of the pattern between them can be explained by the geometry and forces of the material world.
Where the evidence stands: Special relativity and electron interference are experimentally established. The proposed exact reduction of quantum interference to classical plasma-lensing trajectories remains a research hypothesis, not a proven replacement for quantum mechanics.
