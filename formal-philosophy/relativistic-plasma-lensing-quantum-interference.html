Relativistic Plasma-Lensing Framework for Quantum Interference

Analytical unifications, classical action branches, electrostatic scattering, and catastrophe optics. An unreviewed theoretical proposal.

Saumya Bhandari · October 2026 · Research manuscript (unreviewed)
Editorial status. This HTML manuscript preserves the submitted framework and its main mathematical proposals in formal form while correcting malformed mathematical notation. Its claimed exact classical-to-quantum equivalence is not established. The distinctions below between proven identities, approximations and additional hypotheses are essential.

← Read the accessible illustrated introduction first.

Abstract

This manuscript investigates whether charged-particle interactions with a structured, relativistically transformed slit plate might generate classical focusing caustics that contribute to observed electron-screen intensity patterns. We assemble Lorentz kinematics, relativistic charged-particle dynamics, classical Hamilton–Jacobi propagation, Van Vleck determinants, paraxial wave equations and diffraction catastrophes into a candidate plasma-lensing picture. We then state the stronger exact-emergence hypothesis and its mathematical and experimental obstacles. In particular, branchwise vanishing of a quantum potential is not a general demonstration that classical probability densities reproduce quantum interference.

1. Relativistic geometry and temporal stasis

Consider a plate of rest-frame thickness \(L\) and a charge incident with relative velocity \(v\). In the charge's inertial frame, its longitudinal thickness is \(L'=L/\gamma\), with \(\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\). The particle-frame crossing interval is therefore \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=L/(\gamma v)\) under the idealized constant-speed geometry. The comparison \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}\ll\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}\) motivates a quasi-static approximation for an environmental degree of freedom with correlation time \(\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}\).

\[L'=\frac{L}{\gamma},\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{L}{\gamma v},\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}\]

Such a separation of time scales is not a demonstration that thermal disorder vanishes. A single frozen configuration can contain positional disorder and scatter an incident electron. A frame transformation also transforms all components of the electromagnetic field tensor; it does not generally produce a static, purely electrostatic lattice.

2. Effective solid-state plasma response

The working hypothesis adds the static lattice contribution \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) to an induced collective response \(\Phi_{\mathrm{plasma}}\). Electron gases in solids can support plasmons and polarization effects, but their coupling to the beam and coherence must be derived from an appropriate material response rather than assumed to be a perfectly periodic wakefield. In the special case of scalar electrostatic potential \(\Phi\), a classical charge satisfies the relativistic Lorentz equation.

\[\frac{d(\gamma m\mathbf v)}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\qquad \mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\]

The proposed scalar-only approximation requires quantitative justification for neglecting magnetic fields, loss, recoil and stochastic scattering. It cannot replace the full relativistic force equation by \(m\,d\mathbf v/dt=-q\nabla\Phi\) at arbitrary speed.

3. Dirac equation and paraxial envelope

The appropriate starting point for a relativistic spin-half charge in an external classical electromagnetic field is the minimally coupled Dirac equation. A high-energy forward-moving beam can, subject to spin, field-strength and slowly varying envelope assumptions, admit a paraxial approximation. This is an approximation to an underlying quantum wave equation, rather than an exact conversion to classical mechanics.

\[i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\left[c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A)+\beta mc^2+q\Phi\right]\Psi\]

A schematic paraxial propagation law has the form \(i\hbar v\,\partial_z\phi=\hat H_\perp\phi\); the precise transverse operator and potential prefactors must be derived for the specified fields and approximations. No universal coherence guarantee follows solely from a bounded coupling prefactor.

4. Hamilton–Jacobi transport and the Bohm potential

The polar substitution \(\psi=\sqrt\rho\exp(iS/\hbar)\) in the Schrödinger equation gives a continuity equation and an exact quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The latter differs from the classical Hamilton–Jacobi equation by the quantum potential.

\[\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\frac{\nabla S}{m}\right)=0\]
\[\partial_tS+\frac{|\nabla S|^2}{2m}+V+Q=0,\qquad Q=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\nabla^2\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}\]

The proposal appeals to point-source Feynman kernels. For a harmonic oscillator, the exact propagator has a position-independent modulus away from singular times; its branch amplitude therefore gives \(Q=0\) where defined. That property is special to quadratic actions with their corresponding prefactors and does not establish \(Q=0\) for arbitrary slit scattering. After coherent superposition, \(Q[\sum_j\psi_j]\) is generally nonzero. Thus the Vattay-type objection survives as a question about generic branch and global evolution.

5. Action branches and the Van Vleck determinant

The classical action \(S_j(\mathbf q_f,\mathbf q_i;t)\) indexes stationary classical paths when such paths exist. Van Vleck semiclassical propagation weights their contributions using a mixed second derivative of the action. In standard semiclassical theory the propagator also contains a phase and a Maslov index that accounts for caustic crossings.

\[\Delta_j=\left|\det\!\left(-\frac{\partial^2 S_j}{\partial\mathbf q_f\partial\mathbf q_i}\right)\right|\]
\[K_{\mathrm{sc}}\sim\sum_j\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_j}}{(2\pi i\hbar)^{d/2}}\exp\!\left(\frac{iS_j}{\hbar}-\frac{i\pi\mu_j}{2}\right)\]

This is a semiclassical amplitude formula, not an exact identity for arbitrary interactions. At caustics the isolated stationary-phase terms become singular, motivating uniform diffraction approximations.

6. Dark fringes and action-phase interference

Writing \(\psi_j=\sqrt{\rho_j}e^{iS_j/\hbar}\) gives interference algebra after amplitudes, rather than probabilities, are added. The correct unordered-pair expression is:

\[\left|\sum_j\psi_j\right|^2=\sum_j\rho_j+2\sum_{j

The cosine cross-terms permit dark regions under suitable phase and amplitude conditions. Their algebra is exact once the complex-amplitude model is adopted. But a distribution of classical point particles, without this additional amplitude-composition rule, gives a nonnegative sum of path probabilities. The postulate that the complex field is physically observable through its squared modulus therefore requires independent derivation for an exact classical-emergence claim.

7. Catastrophe optics: fold and cusp caustics

Where stationary rays coalesce, diffraction integrals have universal local forms. A fold is governed by an Airy integral and a cusp by a Pearcey integral. These connections make classical focusing a valuable geometric explanation of certain intensity structures. They neither imply that every double-slit fringe is a caustic nor remove the phase-superposition requirement.

\[\mathrm{Ai}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(t^3/3+xt)}\,dt\]
\[\mathrm{Pe}(a,b)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(t^4/4+at^2/2+bt)}\,dt\]

8. Neutral particles and the proposed vacuum sheath

The manuscript hypothesizes that boundary-induced vacuum polarization provides a deterministic lens for neutral neutrons and large molecules. It supplies no derived field strength, material susceptibility, interaction potential, or resulting fringe spacing. Neutral-particle and grating diffraction occur in regimes where this hypothetical sheath has not been established as the dominant mechanism. The universal version of the proposal is therefore open, not proven.

9. Single-particle interference and Bell correlations

A many-valued classical action can encode alternative paths in a geometrical calculation, but this alone does not specify a deterministic guidance law reproducing single-event statistics. Likewise, the influence of both slit boundaries on a one-particle wave is not equivalent to reproducing a Bell-inequality violation between spacelike separated measurements. Any Bell-compatible hidden-variable extension must construct joint measurement statistics and show their compatibility with experimental correlations and no-signalling.

10. Requirements for an exact proof

The substantive hypothesis can be expressed as an equivalence claim between a normalized classical output density \(p_{\mathrm{cl}}(x)\), calculated only from independently defined relativistic dynamics, and the full quantum screen distribution:

\[p_{\mathrm{cl}}(x;L,v,W,\ldots)\stackrel{?}{=}\frac{|\Psi_{\mathrm{QM}}(x;L,v,W,\ldots)|^2}{\int |\Psi_{\mathrm{QM}}(x')|^2\,dx'}\]

To establish this equality, the framework must specify an initial classical ensemble, solve its complete material interaction, independently derive the complex phase and amplitude-composition rule (or avoid requiring them), calculate the full diffraction profile including zeros, and compare parameter-dependent predictions with experiment. Coherent quantum interference is already observed in nonrelativistic and neutral systems; the claimed universal mechanism must reproduce these cases quantitatively.

11. Conclusion

The relativistic plasma-lensing proposal brings together several established mathematical tools in a concrete physical hypothesis: an electron beam encounters material fields that can refract classical trajectories and potentially create caustics. The conjecture that this mechanism exactly generates all quantum interference, with Planck's constant and the Born measure emerging from classical mechanics alone, remains unproven. The investigation is therefore presented as an independent theoretical proposal, not as an accepted theorem.
